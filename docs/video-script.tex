\begin{note}
\textbf{How this works.} You never speak. Each segment has two halves: an orange
\textbf{WHAT TO SHOW} block telling you exactly what to put on screen and when,
and a green \textbf{NARRATION} block containing the words for the AI voice.

The narration is already written \emph{for speech synthesis} --- H\,T\,T\,P
status codes are spelled ``four oh nine'' so they are not read as ``four
hundred and nine'', acronyms are spaced so they are read as letters, and there
are no symbols an engine has to guess at. Do not tidy it back into normal
punctuation; it will sound worse.

Every block is also saved as a plain text file under
\texttt{docs/narration/}, which is far easier to paste from than a PDF.
\end{note}

\section{Production workflow}

\textbf{Generate the audio first, then record the screen while listening to it.}
That is the whole trick --- your clicks land in sync naturally, and you never
have to cut picture to match sound afterwards.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Generate nine audio clips}, one per segment, from the files in
        \texttt{docs/narration/}. Keep them separate rather than generating one
        long file: if segment five needs a retake you re-render thirty seconds,
        not nine minutes.
  \item \textbf{Listen to each clip once} and note where the emphasis falls.
  \item \textbf{Record the screen} for one segment at a time, playing that
        segment's audio in headphones so your actions track the words.
  \item \textbf{Assemble} the nine picture clips against the nine audio clips on
        a timeline. Because each pair was recorded together they line up
        end to end with no trimming.
  \item \textbf{Export} 1920$\times$1080. Check the total is under ten minutes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Voice settings that matter}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item Pick a \textbf{calm, mid-paced} voice. Anything energetic reads as a
        sales pitch and undercuts a technical walkthrough.
  \item Target \textbf{150--160 words per minute}. The timings in this document
        assume 155. Much faster and segment five becomes hard to follow.
  \item \textbf{Keep one voice for all nine clips}, and generate them in one
        session --- some tools drift in tone between sessions.
  \item If a number is misread, spell it phonetically in the text and
        regenerate. Do not fix it by slowing the whole clip down.
\end{itemize}

\begin{note}
\textbf{Full script in one file.} \texttt{docs/narration/full-script.txt}
contains all nine segments concatenated, if you would rather generate a single
audio file and cut it. Segment-by-segment is still recommended.
\end{note}

\begin{note}
\textbf{Run \texttt{demo:reset} before every take, and hard-reload after it.}
The seed is idempotent by upsert, so it never deletes bookings --- a re-seed
will \emph{not} free up a slot you already booked. And the slot listing is
cached for fifteen seconds, so a soft refresh can still show stale state.

The opening shot depends on Dr Mehta's first three slots being free: they are
the brief's worked example, ten o'clock, ten twenty, ten forty.
\end{note}

This is Clinzo, a doctor slot scheduling backend built for the backend

The problem sounds simple. A doctor says: I work Mondays, ten in the morning

\voicehdr{02-stack}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=tts]
The stack is Node twenty two, TypeScript in strict mode, Express, PostgreSQL
through Prisma, Redis for locking, caching and rate limiting, and Bull M Q for
background jobs. Everything is containerised, with GitHub Actions running the
full test suite on every push.

That is about ten thousand eight hundred lines of TypeScript, and three
thousand more of tests.

The architecture is layered, and strict about it. Controllers translate H T T P
and nothing else. Every real decision lives in the service layer. Everything
that touches Postgres goes through a repository.

The slot generator is deliberately pure. No database, no clock, no input or
output at all. Availability in, slots out. That is what makes the hardest
arithmetic in the system directly testable.
\end{lstlisting}

\voicehdr{03-slots-timezones}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=tts]
This is a demo console, served by the same Express app. Plain HTML and
JavaScript, no framework and no build step. It exists to make the backend's
behaviour visible.

Doctor Anaya Mehta works in Asia Kolkata. Fifteen minute consultations, with a
five minute buffer between them.

Look at the first three slots. Ten o'clock. Ten twenty. Ten forty. Fifteen
minutes of appointment plus five minutes of buffer, so they start twenty
minutes apart. That is the worked example from the assessment brief, and it
comes straight out of the generator.

Every slot is stored as a single U T C instant. But it is rendered three ways:
the U T C instant, the viewer's local time, and the doctor's local time. All
three are computed by the server, not by the browser.

Switching the viewing timezone to New York shows the same instants in New York
time, with the doctor's Kolkata time alongside. Because when a patient in New
York and a doctor in India both talk about the ten forty appointment, they have
to mean the same moment.

Two bugs here are worth naming. Luxon's plus minutes function measures elapsed
time, so on a spring forward day, midnight plus a hundred and eighty minutes is
four in the morning, not three. Every slot after the transition was silently
shifting by an hour. A test caught it.

And a validator accepted the string I S T. The timezone database says that
means Israel. Almost everyone typing it means India. That is a three and a half
hour error on every appointment.
\end{lstlisting}

\voicehdr{04-booking}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=tts]
Signing in as a patient. Authentication is J W T, with short lived access
tokens and refresh token rotation. If a refresh token is ever replayed, which
means it was stolen, the entire session family is revoked.

Booking the ten o'clock slot returns a confirmation code. The panel on the
right shows every A P I call, with its status, its timing and its request id.
Every response carries that request id, so a user report maps to exactly one
log line.

A hold is different from a booking. It is the checkout window, a short
reservation while a patient finishes paying. The countdown is enforced by the
server, and when it expires the slot returns to the pool on its own. Nothing
has to succeed for that to happen.

Rescheduling moves the appointment to a new slot and keeps the same booking id.
It is one atomic operation, deliberately not a cancel followed by a rebook,
because that would leave a window with no appointment at all.

And cancelling puts the slot straight back into public availability.
\end{lstlisting}

\voicehdr{05-concurrency}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=tts]
This is the core of the assessment.

Three layers guard a booking. A Redis distributed lock. A select for update row
lock inside the transaction. And a partial unique index in Postgres. Only the
third one is the guarantee.

Here is the race. Twelve different signed in patients, twelve booking requests
for one single slot, released simultaneously.

One, two oh one Created. Eleven, four oh nine Conflicts. About a hundred and
thirty milliseconds.

They are twelve separate accounts deliberately. The booking rate limiter allows
thirty requests per minute per user, so firing all twelve from one account
would demonstrate the rate limiter instead of the concurrency control.

Now the claim worth defending. The Redis lock is not what makes this correct.

A lease based distributed lock cannot be made safe. If a process pauses, for a
long garbage collection say, past the lock's expiry, it wakes up believing it
still holds a lock that Redis has already handed to somebody else. And then it
writes. Treating Redlock as a correctness mechanism is precisely how systems
double book.

So the lock here is a throughput optimisation. It stops a hundred contenders
from each holding a database connection while queueing on the same row. It is
not the guarantee.

And that can be proven, by removing it. This test stubs the lock out entirely,
with no Redis coordination at all, fires the contenders, and still asserts
exactly one winner. It passes. That test is the actual proof.

What holds the line is this, in the migration. A partial unique index. Unique
on slot id, where the status is confirmed. One confirmed booking per slot.

Postgres refuses the second write regardless of what the application code
believes, regardless of how many replicas are running, and regardless of
whether Redis is even up.

The where clause matters too. It is scoped to confirmed bookings only, which is
exactly why a cancelled booking frees the slot to be booked once more.
\end{lstlisting}

\voicehdr{06-retroactive}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=tts]
The hardest design question in the system.

A doctor publishes Monday, ten to six. A patient books three in the afternoon.
Then the doctor shrinks Monday to ten until two. There is now a confirmed
appointment sitting outside the doctor's stated availability.

Auto cancelling the booking silently breaks a commitment between two people.
Rejecting the change lets one appointment freeze an entire schedule. The third
option is to keep the booking and report the conflict.

That is what this does. Future unbooked slots outside the new window are
blocked. Future booked slots are left untouched and returned as orphaned
bookings. Past slots are never modified. The inconsistency is transient,
visible, and actionable.
\end{lstlisting}

\voicehdr{07-outbox}
\begin{lstlisting}[style=tts]
When a booking is created, a notification has to go out. Writing the booking,
committing, and then publishing the event means that if the process dies in
between, the appointment exists and the patient is never told.

So the event is written to an outbox table, inside the same transaction as the
booking. Either both land or neither does. A relay then polls that table using
skip locked, and publishes from there. That gives at least once delivery with
idempotent consumers, which is far easier to get right than distributed
atomicity.

One thing stated plainly: the final delivery hop to an email provider is
stubbed. It logs instead of sending. Everything around it is real.
\end{lstlisting}
